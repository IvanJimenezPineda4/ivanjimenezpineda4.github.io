The Machinist’s Hammer

A multi-material alloy steel hammer, machined, heat treated, and hardness tested

Context Harvey Mudd machine shop project. Individual build, Mar – Apr 2025.

My Role Everything: machining, heat treatment, hardness testing, finishing, and the display stand.

Tools Mill · Lathe · Vertical bandsaw · Belt sander · Sand blaster · Heat-treat oven · Rockwell tester · Laser cutter · 3D printer

Status Completed
Build Analyze

Context

The Harvey Mudd machine shop’s hammer project takes a hammer from raw alloy steel stock to a finished, working tool. It’s a crash course in precision machining and materials processing. I now train new shop users through this same project every semester as a Machine Shop Proctor.

Parameters: a multi-material hammer made from AISI 4340 and AISI 1015 steel, with a hard face, a soft face, a handle, and a stand. The hard face had to be heat treated to a target hardness.

My Role

I built the hammer myself: I machined every steel component, heat treated and tested the hard face, and finished and assembled it with its stand.

What I Built

How I Built It

  1. Rough and finish the parts on the mill and lathe, using the vertical bandsaw and belt sander for stock prep and edges.
  2. Heat treat the hard face at 857 °C to harden it.
  3. Verify the hardness on a Rockwell tester, reading RC 43–48.
  4. Finish the surfaces with the sand blaster.
  5. Make the stand with the laser cutter (engraved) and the 3D printer, then assemble.

Why I Did It This Way

Heat treat the hard face

Why: A hardened striking face resists denting and mushrooming when it hits other metal, so the hammer lasts.

Measure, don’t assume

Why: The Rockwell test confirms the heat treatment actually reached the target hardness (RC 43–48) instead of trusting the oven settings.

Hard face + soft face

Why: One head does two jobs: the hard face for striking, and the soft face for tapping parts into place without marring them.
